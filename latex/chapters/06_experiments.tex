\chapter{Graph-Design Screening}
\label{ch:experiments}

This chapter reports
six graph-design screens (Screens A--F). All screens are evaluated on
seed-0. They ask whether
a specific choice in the actor of Chapter~\ref{ch:methods} changes survival on held-out chronics.
Screens A--D evaluate busbar graphs, Screen E evaluates the
disaggregated graph, and Screen F evaluates the disaggregated line-node graph
with a transferable candidate-action interface. Comparisons are controlled
within each screen; results across screens document the progression of the
design rather than a single cumulative ablation. \latestrevision{Screens A--E form the
source-representation branch later tested through frozen-encoder transfer,
whereas Screen F forms the complete-policy transfer branch.}

\subsection{Endpoint statistics}
\label{subsec:screening-endpoint-statistics}

During training, checkpoints are evaluated through rollouts on
the training split. The checkpoint with the highest mean survival in these
training-rollout evaluations is retained. This selected checkpoint is then
tested on the complete held-out test set of 201 chronics, and that full-test
score is reported in the screening results. Every run reaches the 15M-step
budget, and all screens report one seed per cell.

\subsection{Compute}
\label{subsec:screening-budget-alignment}

The campaigns were executed on JED (CPU) and IZAR (GPU). Hardware and execution setup were held fixed within each factorial to ensure fair comparisons. Since the screens evaluate exactly one training seed per cell, performance differences are descriptive rather than estimates of between-seed effects.
\\
\\
Training throughput is dominated by stepping the 72 Grid2Op environments rather than the model's forward and backward passes. For example, in Screen B (Section~\ref{sec:screen-eepth-width}), tripling the actor parameter count from 83.6k to 244.9k has a negligible effect on wall-clock time. Parameter count is therefore not the bottleneck when selecting architectures.

\section{\texorpdfstring{Screen A: Message-Passing Operator}{Screen A: Message-Passing Operator}}
\label{sec:preliminary-gine-gat-screen}

\subsection*{Question}

Screen A compared GAT
and GINE as the message-passing operator. The purpose was simply to select one
operator for the remaining experiments.

\subsection*{Design}

Both actors used the busbar graph, two message-passing layers,
a hidden width of 128, and the ordinary mean graph readout over visible
busbars. The optimization budget and evaluation protocol were otherwise held
fixed. Table~\ref{tab:preliminary-gine-gat} reports the seed-0 full-test score
of the best checkpoint over the 201 test chronics.

GINE outperforms GAT by 3.66 percentage points under this matched

theoretical expectations, as GINE is known to be highly expressive for distinguishing
different graph topologies (aligning with the Weisfeiler-Lehman graph isomorphism test).
\\
\\
\noindent\textbf{Role downstream.} GINE is kept as the common encoder for all subsequent screens.

\section{Screen B: Encoder Depth and Width}
\label{sec:screen-eepth-width}

\subsection*{Question}

This screen measures the effect of message-passing depth $K$ and encoder width $d_h$. On the busbar graph, depth
controls reach: one layer crosses one transmission-line edge, while two layers
let each busbar combine information across paths of two physical edges. The
graph-level readout then pools all valid node embeddings, so $K$ need not equal
the diameter of the local graph. Width controls how much information the node
and pooled embeddings can retain.
\\
\\
The result applies directly only to the busbar schema; using the same pair for
graphs with explicit asset or line nodes remains an assumption.

\subsection*{Design}

A complete $3\times4$ grid at seed 0, yielding 12 runs. This screen is executed entirely on the busbar graph representation.
Table~\ref{tab:depth-width-design} lists the screened parameters and their
levels. The graph output dimension is matched to the hidden dimension, so a
cell varies one width rather than two. Every other setting is inherited from
the reference configuration of
Section~\ref{sec:screening-common-protocol}.

Rather than providing a general scaling law, this screen instead serves to identify a single robust operating point for subsequent experiments.

candidate-action models of Screen F.

\section{Screen C: Graph Readout Scope and Reducer}
\label{sec:screen-fncoder-pooling}

\subsection*{Question}

The local graph may contain nodes that provide message-passing
context without being equally useful in the final graph summary. Should the graph readout include every visible node, only controlled nodes, only energized nodes, or the
intersection of controlled and energized nodes? For each scope, should the
valid embeddings be averaged or reduced by a per-channel maximum?
\\
\\
The scope mask is applied only at graph readout. Context nodes remain in the
message-passing graph, so this screen changes which embeddings form the policy
summary without changing what information can propagate to a controlled node.

\subsection*{Design}

A $4\times2$ factorial crosses four readout scopes, namely all visible,
controlled, energized, and controlled $\cap$ energized, with mean and max reduction at
seed 0. All cells use GINE with $K=2$ and $d_h=128$ on the busbar
graph. Seven cells are new; the all-visible/mean cell reuses
\texttt{mp2\_h128} from Screen B because it is exactly the same configuration.

\textbf{Energized-node readout leads with both reducers.} Its mean-readout cell reaches 98.93\% and its max-readout cell reaches 99.39\%, only 0.46 points apart. Relative to using all visible nodes at graph readout, the energized-only scope improves survival by 4.21 points under mean and 3.08 points under max.
\\
\\
\textbf{Max is directionally consistent across scopes.} For every matched scope, maximum graph readout exceeds mean graph readout on the full test. The gain ranges from 0.19 points (controlled nodes) to 5.01 points (controlled-and-energized intersection). The magnitude depends strongly on scope, but the positive direction does not.
\\
\\
\textbf{Why carry energized/max forward.} It combines the best scope with the reducer that wins every within-scope full-test comparison. Table~\ref{tab:encoder-pooling-training-curves} also identifies it as the strongest overall training curve: it is the earliest to cross 90\% (3.40M steps) and has the highest time-weighted curve mean (79.26\%). While energized/mean has slightly better late-window variance, \texttt{energized\_max} has the strongest full-test endpoint (99.39\%) and is the most consistently supported operating point overall. 
\\
\\
\noindent\textbf{Role downstream.} Energized/max is retained
as the global readout of the fixed-head encoders used in the encoder-transfer
branch. We fix it for Screens D and E to prevent readout from becoming an additional confounding factor.

\section{Screen D: Structural Augmentations of the Busbar Graph}
\label{sec:screen-cus-structure}

\subsection*{Question}

Three optional augmentations add computational structure to the busbar graph
without adding any physical measurement: same-substation edges ($e$), which let
the busbars of one substation exchange messages directly; substation-summary
nodes ($n$), which insert one learned hub per substation; and virtual-node
readout ($v$), which replaces the mean graph readout with a learned graph-summary node.
Each is motivated by the same intuition, shorten the path between related
busbars, so the question is whether any of them earns its cost, and whether
they compose.

\subsection*{Design}

A complete $2^3$ factorial at seed 0, 8 runs. The graph type is
busbar throughout and the baseline cell is the unaugmented
\texttt{e0n0v0}. It has the same $K=2$, width-128, GINE/\texttt{energized\_max} design as
the reference from Screen C. Cells are named by their three switches, so
\texttt{e1n0v1} means same-substation edges on, summary nodes off, virtual-node
readout on.

\subsection*{Result}

The complete factorial result is shown in
Figure~\ref{fig:structure-cube}.

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.86\linewidth]{figures/gs_s2_structure_bar.png}
  \caption{Screen D. The factorial results as a bar plot, with the baseline \texttt{e0n0v0} highlighted.}
  \label{fig:structure-cube}
\end{figure}

\subsection*{Reading}

\textbf{Modest gains do not justify increased complexity.} While same-substation edges slightly improve survival (reaching 97.19\% vs. the 95.45\% baseline\footnote{This \texttt{e0n0v0} baseline matches the best Screen C cell mathematically, but differing hardware between campaigns led to trajectory divergence.}), this minor benefit degrades erratically when combined with hierarchical factors. Furthermore, virtual-node readouts prove highly destructive (dropping survival to 18--77\%), likely because stacking multiple aggregation hubs restricts information flow within our two-layer ($K=2$) encoder. Consequently, we retain the simple, unaugmented busbar graph for subsequent stages.
\\
\\
\noindent\textbf{Role downstream.} Screen D therefore selects the unaugmented
\texttt{e0n0v0} encoder; the summary-node and virtual-readout variants are
discarded. The later frozen-encoder experiment includes \texttt{e1n0v0} only as
a controlled comparator, not as the selected architecture. This paired test
asks whether its small source-grid gain survives a change of grid and therefore
whether the added edge structure contains more reusable information.

\section{Screen E: Generator and Load Message Direction}
\label{sec:screen-message-direction}

\subsection*{Question}

In the disaggregated graph, each generator and load is a node attached to its
busbar. Section~\ref{sec:heterogeneous-graph-representation} leaves the
direction of that attachment open: the asset may send messages to the busbar,
receive them from it, or both. The two directions are not symmetric in effect,
because the \texttt{energized\_max} readout pools
asset nodes as well as busbars. Does the choice matter, and do the generator
and load relations behave independently?

\subsection*{Design}

A complete $3\times3$ factorial over the generator and load directions at seed 0: 9 runs.
The graph type is disaggregated throughout; the line and summary
directions stay \texttt{bidirectional} and are inert for this schema. The
encoder matches the reference configuration: GINE, two layers, 128
features, \texttt{energized\_max} readout, no augmentations, so the only screened factors are
the two attachment directions. The baseline cell is bidirectional on both
relations. Directions are abbreviated \texttt{bi} (bidirectional), \texttt{a2b}
(asset to busbar), and \texttt{b2a} (busbar to asset).

\subsection*{Result}

The complete endpoint ranking is shown in
Figure~\ref{fig:direction-seed-heatmap}.

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.96\linewidth]{figures/gs_hmd_direction_seed_heatmap.png}
  \caption{Screen E. Full-test mean survival of the best checkpoint by generator
  direction (rows) and load direction (columns). The baseline cell is
  \texttt{bi}/\texttt{bi}.}
  \label{fig:direction-seed-heatmap}
\end{figure}

\subsection*{Reading}

\textbf{Asymmetric and bidirectional flows are optimal.} The best configuration uses \texttt{a2b} (asset-to-busbar) for generators and \texttt{b2a} (busbar-to-asset) for loads (99.12\% survival), closely followed by the fully bidirectional configuration (\texttt{bi}/\texttt{bi}) at 99.02\% survival. Other symmetric or reversed configurations perform significantly worse. The success of the asymmetric \texttt{gen\_a2b}/\texttt{load\_b2a} flow makes physical sense: because the \texttt{energized\_max} graph readout includes generator and load nodes, pushing generator information inward to the busbars and pulling busbar information outward to the loads allows the network to correctly route production capacity into the core topology while load nodes contextualize their demand. This prevents the dilution of the pooled graph embedding that occurs when edges incorrectly blur these distinct physical roles.
\\
\\
\noindent\textbf{Role downstream.} Both the best configurationsare retained for frozen-encoder transfer: \texttt{gbi\_lbi}
and \texttt{ga2b\_lb2a}. Their 0.10-point source-grid gap is too small at one
seed to justify declaring a universal direction, so the WCCI experiment tests
whether either relation pattern produces more reusable features.

\section{Screen F: Candidate-Action Scoring}
\label{sec:screen-f-cas}

\subsection*{Question}
The candidate-action head of Section~\ref{sec:candidate-action-scoring} scores
every action from the encoded nodes it modifies rather than from a fixed-width
layer over an action index. Three choices in that head are unresolved: how
candidate pooling reduces the touched nodes, whether the static action descriptor is appended to
the score, and whether the idle action gets its own head. This screen varies all
three.

\subsection*{Design}
A $2^3$ factorial on \texttt{bus14}, seed $0$, eight runs. Candidate pooling is
\texttt{mean} or \texttt{typed\_mean}; the action descriptor is appended
(\texttt{f1}) or withheld (\texttt{f0}); the idle action is scored by a
dedicated head (\texttt{a0h1}) or by the shared scorer (\texttt{a0h0}). Every
run uses the disaggregated line-node graph, a two-layer GINE
encoder of width $128$, and the 15M-step budget of
Section~\ref{sec:screening-common-protocol}.

checkpoint over the 201 held-out chronics.

\subsection*{Reading}
\textbf{The candidate-action head is a viable actor.} Six of the eight cells reach $98.2\%$ or better and three exceed
$99\%$. No single factor strictly separates the performance. The apparent marginal gaps, such as \texttt{mean} at
$98.9\%$ against \texttt{typed\_mean} at $87.0\%$, and \texttt{f0} at $97.3\%$
against \texttt{f1} at $88.6\%$, are driven by two low-performing interaction cells; removing the
single failure at $57.84\%$ brings the candidate-pooling gap to about a point. At one seed,
the defensible statement is that the head works and that the three choices do
not cleanly order themselves. Although joint-mean pooling and
withholding the descriptor avoid the two weakest cells on \texttt{bus14}, this
single-seed evidence is not sufficient to select one variant. All eight are
therefore retained: choices that are weakly separated on the source grid may
differentiate more clearly when transferred to the larger and more difficult
WCCI grid.
\\
\\
\noindent\textbf{Role downstream.} Screen F supplies the
complete-policy branch of Chapter~\ref{ch:screen-f-transfer}. All eight source
actors are evaluated after transfer, with each encoder and shared candidate
scorer reused together.
