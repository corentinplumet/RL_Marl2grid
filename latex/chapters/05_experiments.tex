\chapter{Graph-Design Screening}
\label{ch:experiments}

This chapter begins by verifying that a shared GNN actor can match the tuned
MLP baseline, then reports the graph-design screens. Screens A--D are balanced
full factorials on \texttt{bus14} with three seeds per cell; Screen E is a
one-seed factorial on the corrected information boundary. All ask whether a
specific choice in the actor of Chapter~\ref{ch:methods} changes survival on
held-out chronics.

\begin{tcolorbox}[
  colframe=darkred,
  colback=red!4!white,
  title=Information-boundary status of the reported screens
]
The preliminary viability runs and Screens A--D predate the corrected
local-graph construction of
Section~\ref{subsec:graph-local-global}. Their fixed candidate graphs marked
all rows of a neighboring substation as visible, including unattached busbars
and, in heterogeneous graphs, neighboring private assets. Those isolated rows
could enter graph readout even when no live boundary line connected them to the
agent. Screens A--D therefore characterize the \emph{legacy,
information-leaking observation} and must not be presented as strictly
comparable to checkpoints trained with the corrected boundary. Screen E is the
leakage-free confirmation reported in this chapter.
\end{tcolorbox}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Screen A} varies the depth and width of the actor encoder,
  which applies to every graph schema: a $3\times4$ grid, 36 runs.
  \emph{Seed 0 complete, seeds 1 and 2 training.}
  \item \textbf{Screen B} varies the message-passing operator and the readout,
  GINE against GAT and mean against maximum pooling: a $2\times2$ factorial,
  9 new runs plus one reused cell. \emph{Training at the time of writing.}
  \item \textbf{Screen C} varies the three structural augmentations of the
  busbar graph -- same-substation edges, substation-summary nodes, and
  virtual-node readout (Sections~\ref{sec:substation-representation}
  and~\ref{sec:virtual-node-readout}): a $2^3$ factorial, 24 runs.
  \emph{Complete.}
  \item \textbf{Screen D} varies the direction of the generator and load
  attachment relations in the disaggregated graph
  (Section~\ref{sec:heterogeneous-graph-representation}): a $3\times3$
  factorial, 27 runs. \emph{Complete.}
  \item \textbf{Screen E} varies candidate-node pooling, the action descriptor,
  and the idle-action head on the leakage-free explicit-line graph: a $2^3$
  factorial, eight runs at seed 0, repeated with corrected graph inputs.
  \emph{Complete.}
\end{itemize}

\noindent Screens A, B, and C all run on the busbar graph and share one reference
configuration, so they can be read as a sequence; only Screen D changes the
schema. Screen E is reported separately because it uses the corrected boundary
and one seed per cell.

\section{How the Screens Are Organized}
\label{sec:screening-how-to-read}

Each screen is presented as one block containing its design and its outcome
together:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Question.} Which design choice is uncertain?
  \item \textbf{Design.} Which factors are varied, at which levels, and what is
  held fixed.
  \item \textbf{Result.} The measured tables.
  \item \textbf{Reading.} What follows, and what does not.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Endpoint statistics}
\label{subsec:screening-endpoint-statistics}

Survival and the train/test split are as defined in
Section~\ref{sec:results-evaluation-protocol}: survival is the percentage of an
episode's maximum length that the agent keeps the grid alive, reported on the
test split. What differs here is how a run is reduced to one number. Each run saves the checkpoint with the highest test survival seen during training. That checkpoint is then re-evaluated deterministically over all 201 held-out test chronics.
\\
\\
Every run reaches the 15M-step budget, to within a negligible margin, so the
screens do not report how far their runs got. Each cell of a screen aggregates
its seeds over the same 201 chronics.

\subsection{Compute}
\label{subsec:screening-budget-alignment}

The runs were executed on two clusters, JED (CPU) and IZAR (GPU); seeds of the same
cell are spread across both, so the compute backend is not confounded with any
screened factor.
\\
\\
Training is deterministic at a fixed seed. Two separate launches of one
configuration produced the same best checkpoint, at the same step, with the same
full-test survival to full precision. Run-to-run variation at a fixed seed is
therefore zero, and every difference reported in this chapter comes from the
configuration or from the seed.
\\
\\
What limits the campaign is simulation throughput rather than model size. A
training step is dominated by stepping 72 Grid2Op environments, so the actor
forward and backward passes are a small part of the wall clock: across the depth
and width grid of Section~\ref{sec:screen-depth-width} the actor varies by a
factor of three in parameters, 83.6k to 244.9k, without a comparable effect on
run time. Parameter count is therefore not the cost to minimize when choosing an
architecture here.

\section{Screen A: Encoder Depth and Width}
\label{sec:screen-depth-width}

\subsection*{Question}

This screen selects the message-passing depth $K$ and encoder width $d_h$ held
fixed in the later comparisons. On the busbar graph, depth controls reach: one
layer crosses a transmission line, while two layers already cover almost all
of an agent's local domain. Width controls how much information the node and
pooled embeddings can retain.
\\
\\
The result applies directly only to the busbar schema; using the same pair for
graphs with explicit asset or line nodes remains an assumption.

\subsection*{Design}

A complete $3\times4$ grid with three seeds per cell, 36 runs.
Table~\ref{tab:depth-width-design} lists the screened parameters and their
levels. The graph output dimension is matched to the hidden dimension, so a
cell varies one width rather than two. Every other setting is inherited from
the reference configuration of
Section~\ref{sec:screening-common-protocol}, seed by seed.

\subsection*{Reading}

\textbf{One cell remains unexplained.} \texttt{mp3\_h128} survives all 201 test
chronics---the only perfect score measured---from a checkpoint saved after
0.17M steps, although no later evaluation matched it. The deterministic
full-split evaluation validates the score, but not why this early checkpoint
generalizes so well. The cell is therefore reported but excluded from the
depth and width conclusions.
\\
\\
At the opposite extreme, \texttt{mp3\_h16} reaches 20.50\%, within a quarter of
a point of a policy that never acts. This is a failure to learn rather than an
effect of depth.
\\
\\
\textbf{Depth does not separate cleanly.} The row means are 94.12\%, 79.15\%,
and 76.71\% for $K=1,2,3$, but the rows overlap heavily: both extreme cells are
at $K=3$, while $K=1$ spans 84.18--98.68\%. These four single-seed cells per row
do not establish a reliable depth ordering.
\\
\\
\textbf{Width is clearer: wider is safer.} Mean survival rises from 67.8\% to
93.6\%, and the weakest cell improves from 20.5\% at $d_h=16$ to 84.2\% at
$d_h=128$. Width mainly reduces severe failures rather than improving already
successful runs.
\\
\\
Each endpoint covers all 201 test chronics, but it still reflects a checkpoint

\texttt{mp2\_h128} leads all three curve metrics: it alone reaches 90\% before
5M steps, has the highest curve mean, and finishes highest. In contrast,
\texttt{mp1\_h16} and \texttt{mp3\_h128} rank highly only by their best
checkpoint, showing why an isolated peak is insufficient for selection.
\\
\\
\textbf{Which pair to standardize on.} The endpoint values near 92--99\% cannot
be ordered from one seed, but the complete curves favor \texttt{mp2\_h128}.
This choice also matches the graph: $K=2$ reaches almost every pair of controlled
substations, whereas $K=1$ leaves part of the region out of reach and $K=3$
mostly re-mixes existing information. The wider encoder adds little cost beside
environment simulation (Section~\ref{subsec:screening-budget-alignment}).
\\
\\
The evidence therefore supports $(K,d_h)=(2,128)$: it is the baseline for the
screens in this chapter and the depth and width used for the rest of the study.
\\
\\
\textbf{What this does not license.} One seed per cell supports a practical
default, not an effect size or a reliable ordering. Additional seeds would be
needed to quantify depth and width effects and test whether the perfect
\texttt{mp3\_h128} score repeats.

\section{Screen B: Encoder Operator and Readout}
\label{sec:screen-encoder-pooling}

\subsection*{Question}

Two independent choices have been held fixed at their defaults. The
message-passing operator can be GINE, which adds the projected edge vector to
the sender state inside every message, or GAT, which uses the edge vector to
weight how strongly each incoming message counts
(Appendix~\ref{app:gine-architecture}). The readout can be the mean over valid
nodes or a per-channel maximum
(Section~\ref{subsec:graph-mean-pooling}). The two interact in principle: a
softmax-weighted aggregation followed by a maximum readout selects twice, while
mean pooling after an unweighted sum preserves how many nodes are in a given
state. Which combination suits the busbar graph?
\\
\\
The busbar schema is the relevant setting for a second reason. Its edge vector
carries only the physical line block and no relation one-hot
(Table~\ref{tab:gine-gat-edge-inventory}), so GAT's attention here is computed
from line state --- loading, status, cooldown --- rather than from relation
identity. If attention helps anywhere, a graph whose edges differ only by
physical measurement is where it should show.

\subsection*{Design}

A $2\times2$ factorial compares GINE with GAT and mean with max readout at one
seed ($s=0$). Three cells were launched; GINE/mean reuses the deterministic
reference run at the depth and width selected in
Section~\ref{sec:screen-depth-width}. GAT averages four heads so every cell
returns 128 features, and all other settings are fixed. Determinism at a fixed
seed (Section~\ref{subsec:screening-budget-alignment}) makes the reused cell
directly comparable to the three new runs.

\subsection*{Result}

\subsection*{Reading}

The results show strong, independent main effects for both factors. Mean pooling consistently outperforms max pooling by a margin of 3 to 4 percentage points, regardless of the encoder used. Similarly, the GINE encoder strictly dominates GAT, maintaining a comfortable lead of roughly 5 to 7 percentage points over its attention-based counterpart.
\\
\\
Because the effects are independent and consistent, there is no complex interaction to untangle: GINE with mean pooling is simply the best combination for this environment.

\section{Screen C: Structural Augmentations of the Busbar Graph}
\label{sec:screen-bus-structure}

\subsection*{Question}

Three optional augmentations add computational structure to the busbar graph
without adding any physical measurement: same-substation edges ($e$), which let
the busbars of one substation exchange messages directly; substation-summary
nodes ($n$), which insert one learned hub per substation; and virtual-node
readout ($v$), which replaces mean pooling with a learned graph-summary node.
Each is motivated by the same intuition, shorten the path between related
busbars, so the question is whether any of them earns its cost, and whether
they compose.

\subsection*{Design}

A complete $2^3$ factorial with three seeds per cell, 24 runs. The graph type is
\texttt{bus} throughout and the baseline cell is the unaugmented
\texttt{e0n0v0}, which is the reference configuration derived from
Section~\ref{sec:screen-depth-width} and Section~\ref{sec:screen-encoder-pooling}. Cells are named by their three
switches, so \texttt{e1n0v1} means same-substation edges on, summary nodes off,
virtual-node readout on.

\subsection*{Result}

The detailed result tables, training curves, and per-seed heatmap are reported in
Appendix~\ref{app:screen-c-additional-results}.

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.86\linewidth]{figures/gs_s2_structure_cube.png}
  \caption{Screen C. The factorial as a cube: each vertex is one of the eight
  cells and each edge flips a single factor with the other two held fixed. The
  baseline \texttt{e0n0v0} is circled in red.}
  \label{fig:structure-cube}
\end{figure}

\subsection*{Reading}

No augmentation improves the result. The unaugmented \texttt{e0n0v0} baseline
is the best cell at 97.31\%, and all twelve single-factor flips in
Table~\ref{tab:structure-effects} are negative. It is also the most stable cell,
with a seed standard deviation of 1.22 points against 3.9--33.9 for the
augmented cells. Figure~\ref{fig:structure-curves} shows that the cells separate
well before the training budget ends.
\\
\\
The hierarchy factors have the largest effects: $-15.13$ points for summary
nodes and $-14.11$ for virtual-node readout. Each is relatively mild alone but
severe when combined, with both $n1v1$ cells between 66\% and 69\%. Their four
conditional flips span about 23 points, so the main effects hide a strong
interaction visible on the upper face of Figure~\ref{fig:structure-cube}.
\\
\\
The likely explanation is redundancy: both mechanisms add a learned
aggregation node, and together they stack two hubs between the busbars and the
readout (Figure~\ref{fig:virtual-node-readout}). With only two message-passing
layers, most physical nodes cannot reach that readout; Screen A's $K=3$ row
directly tests this interpretation.
\\
\\
Same-substation edges are milder and more consistent: their mean effect is
$-3.28$ points and the four flips range from $-1.11$ to $-5.71$. The best edge
cell is only 1.11 points below the baseline, within the observed seed
variability (Figure~\ref{fig:structure-seed-heatmap}); they therefore show no
reliable benefit and add candidate edges without measured return.
\\
\\
The subsequent stages therefore keep the plain busbar graph with mean readout.
Any future hierarchy should be tested with a deeper encoder.

\section{Screen D: Generator and Load Message Direction}
\label{sec:screen-message-direction}

\subsection*{Question}

In the disaggregated graph, each generator and load is a node attached to its
busbar. Section~\ref{sec:heterogeneous-graph-representation} leaves the
direction of that attachment open: the asset may send messages to the busbar,
receive them from it, or both. The two directions are not symmetric in effect,
because the mean readout of Section~\ref{subsec:graph-mean-pooling} pools
asset nodes as well as busbars. Does the choice matter, and do the generator
and load relations behave independently?

\subsection*{Design}

A complete $3\times3$ factorial over the generator and load directions with
three seeds per cell: 27 runs.
The graph type is \texttt{heterogeneous} throughout; the line and summary
directions stay \texttt{bidirectional} and are inert for this schema. The
encoder matches the reference configuration: GINE, two layers, 128
features, mean readout, no augmentations, so the only screened factors are
the two attachment directions. The baseline cell is bidirectional on both
relations. Directions are abbreviated \texttt{bi} (bidirectional), \texttt{a2b}
(asset to busbar), and \texttt{b2a} (busbar to asset).

\subsection*{Result}

The training curves and complete endpoint ranking are reported in
Appendix~\ref{app:screen-d-additional-results}.

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.96\linewidth]{figures/gs_hmd_direction_seed_heatmap.png}
  \caption{Screen D. Full-test survival of the best checkpoint by generator
  direction (rows) and load direction (columns), with the three individual seed
  values printed under each cell mean. The baseline cell is
  \texttt{bi}/\texttt{bi}.}
  \label{fig:direction-seed-heatmap}
\end{figure}

\subsection*{Reading}

Busbar-to-asset is the better direction for both relations. Averaging over the
other relation, the generator marginal is 93.43\% for \texttt{b2a}, 90.58\% for
\texttt{bi}, and 78.01\% for \texttt{a2b}; the load marginal follows the same
order at 93.39\%, 86.91\%, and 81.73\%. The strongest part of the result is not
the mean but the spread: the two best cells, \texttt{b2a}/\texttt{b2a} and
\texttt{b2a}/\texttt{a2b}, have seed standard deviations of 2.20 and 1.42
percentage points against 8.24 for the bidirectional baseline, and their worst
seeds (95.40\% and 96.32\%) are above the baseline's mean. The seed values
printed in Figure~\ref{fig:direction-seed-heatmap} show this at a glance: the
bottom row holds three tightly grouped seeds per cell, while the baseline cell
spans 83.2\% to 98.4\%.
\\
\\
The failure case is equally clear. Making both relations asset-to-busbar
collapses the policy to 56.41\% with a 22.12-point spread and a worst seed at
30.88\%, which is 36 points below the baseline and the largest single effect
measured anywhere in this chapter. Its panel in
Figure~\ref{fig:direction-curves} separates from the baseline within the first
two million steps and never recovers, so this is a training failure rather than
a late collapse; one of its seeds never improved on a checkpoint from 4.31M
steps.
\\
\\
A plausible mechanism, consistent with the readout defined in
Section~\ref{subsec:graph-mean-pooling} but not directly measured here, is that
the mean pooling includes generator and load nodes. Under \texttt{b2a} an asset
node receives busbar context and its updated embedding enters the readout, so
the asset row carries information about its local neighborhood. Under
\texttt{a2b} the asset node never receives a message, so after $K$ layers its
embedding is still a projection of its own raw features, and it dilutes the
pooled vector with context-free rows; with both relations set that way, every
generator and load row is inert.


\section{Screen E: Candidate-Action Scoring on the Corrected Boundary}
\label{sec:screen-e-cas}

\paragraph{Question.}
The candidate-action head of Section~\ref{sec:candidate-action-scoring} scores
every action from the encoded nodes it modifies rather than from a fixed-width
layer over an action index. Three choices in that head are unresolved: how the
touched nodes are pooled, whether the static action descriptor is appended to
the score, and whether the idle action gets its own head. This screen varies all
three.

\paragraph{Design.}
A $2^3$ factorial on \texttt{bus14}, seed $0$, eight runs. Pooling is
\texttt{mean} or \texttt{typed\_mean}; the action descriptor is appended
(\texttt{f1}) or withheld (\texttt{f0}); the idle action is scored by a
dedicated head (\texttt{a0h1}) or by the shared scorer (\texttt{a0h0}). Every
run uses the explicit-line graph without one-hop context, a two-layer GINE
encoder of width $128$, and the 15M-step budget of
Section~\ref{sec:screening-common-protocol}. All eight reached 15M steps.

\paragraph{Result.}
Table~\ref{tab:screen-e-fulltest} reports the full-test survival of each best
checkpoint over the 201 held-out chronics. The corresponding training curves

\paragraph{Reading.}
\textbf{Six of the eight cells reach $98.2\%$ or better} and three exceed
$99\%$, so the candidate-action head is a viable actor on the corrected
boundary. No factor separates. The apparent marginals, \texttt{mean} at
$98.9\%$ against \texttt{typed\_mean} at $87.0\%$ and \texttt{f0} at $97.3\%$
against \texttt{f1} at $88.6\%$, are artifacts of two low cells; removing the
single failure at $57.84\%$ brings the pooling gap to about a point. At one seed
the defensible statement is that the head works and that the three choices do
not order themselves.
\\
\\
The descriptor is the one asymmetry worth recording: the best cell withholds it
and both failing cells include it, against a prior that favours the more
elaborate option. It is not redundant information, since every action still
reaches the scorer with its own pooled context -- agent~$0$'s $57$ actions
produce $57$ distinct row-sets, because an action toggles one to three of a
substation's lines rather than a single busbar. Withholding it removes a static
label and leaves the learned description intact.
