\chapter{What the Shared Encoder Reads}
\label{ch:encoder-inputs}

One encoder serves every agent, and the same encoder is what a transfer study
moves between grids. Its inputs are raw physical quantities, so before either
form of sharing can be interpreted the inputs themselves have to be measured.
This chapter establishes what the encoder consumes, how far those distributions
differ between two grids, which of the differences a network absorbs and which
it does not, and what corrections follow. Without those measurements, a deficit
under transfer could not be told apart from a representation reading
miscalibrated inputs. \latestrevision{Chapter~\ref{ch:wcci-transfer} defines the
target controls, Chapter~\ref{ch:encoder-transfer-bridge} tests frozen
fixed-head encoders with original and corrected preprocessing, and
Chapter~\ref{ch:screen-f-transfer} tests complete-policy transfer.}
\\
\\
The actor and the critic do not face the same problem. The critic consumes the
normalized flat observation and is grid-specific in any case; the encoder reads
raw graph features, with physical scaling and running standardization initially
disabled, so a transferred \texttt{bus14} encoder meets WCCI's raw physical
quantities directly. Only that second distribution is at issue here.


\section{Measurement protocol}
\label{sec:wcci-protocol}

Both environments were rolled out under a do-nothing policy for 6{,}000
environment steps, with a cap of 300 steps per chronic so the sample spreads
over roughly twenty scenarios per grid rather than concentrating in one. At each
step the per-agent local graphs were rebuilt and every visible node recorded,
pooling agents together because a single encoder is shared across them. This
yields 426{,}000 node observations for \texttt{bus14} and 1{,}236{,}000 for
WCCI.
\\
\\
Only nodes are measured, because on this schema only nodes carry measurements:
the nine edge channels are relation indicators, exactly one of which is set on
any active edge, with no continuous content to compare. The transfer runs also
set \texttt{gnn\_include\_neighbors = false}, so every node in a local graph is
controlled and \texttt{domain\_mask} is constant at one throughout.
\\
\\
Four statistics summarize each feature. Writing $F_A$ and $F_B$ for the two
empirical distributions with means $\mu_A,\mu_B$ and standard deviations
$\sigma_A,\sigma_B$, and $\sigma_p=\sqrt{(\sigma_A^2+\sigma_B^2)/2}$ for their
pooled spread:
\begin{align}
  \text{offset} &= \frac{\mu_B-\mu_A}{\sigma_p},
  &\text{scale ratio} &= \frac{\sigma_B}{\sigma_A},
  &\mathrm{KS} &= \sup_x |F_A(x)-F_B(x)| . \label{eq:offset-scale}
\end{align}
{\color{clarif}
\paragraph{Conventions.} $A$ is always \texttt{bus14} and $B$ always WCCI, so an
offset is positive when WCCI sits higher and a scale ratio above one means WCCI
is the wider of the two. $F_A$ and $F_B$ are the empirical CDFs built from the
samples of Section~\ref{sec:wcci-protocol}, and the Kolmogorov--Smirnov
statistic is the largest gap between them: it lies in $[0,1]$ and is drawn in
the last panel of Figure~\ref{fig:wcci-distributions}.}
\\
\\
The pairing is deliberate: the offset is blind to a change in spread and the
scale ratio is blind to a displacement, so a channel is only unproblematic when
both sit near their neutral values, and KS summarises whatever disagreement
remains. Section~\ref{sec:wcci-interpretation} shows why the two failures need
separating.


\section{What the encoder reads}
\label{sec:wcci-features}

The measurements are taken on the disaggregated line-node graph, the schema the
transfer study uses. Its twelve node channels are specified in
Table~\ref{tab:heterogeneous-line-node-features}: four type indicators, the
shared \texttt{p} and \texttt{theta} columns, four line-state columns, the
substation cooldown, and \texttt{domain\_mask}.
\\
\\
Every node type shares one feature vector, so a channel is filled only on the
type that owns it and is zero-filled elsewhere. That makes the sparsity
structural and severe: \texttt{p} and \texttt{theta} carry a value only on
generator and load nodes, which are $24.0\,\%$ of rows on \texttt{bus14} and
$28.7\,\%$ on WCCI, while \texttt{rho} and the other line-state channels are
filled only on transmission-line nodes, $36.6\,\%$ and $36.4\,\%$ of rows. A
statistic taken over all rows therefore mixes a measurement with the zeros of
three other node types, which is why every channel below is reported twice.
\\
\\
Three channels are constant or near-constant in this sample rather than sparse:
the two cooldown columns, which a do-nothing policy never triggers, and
\texttt{timestep\_overflow}. One channel is dimensionless by construction:
\texttt{rho} divides each line's flow by that line's own thermal limit, while
every other physical channel carries the units of the grid it came from.

\noindent Read on all rows the table looks harmless: no channel reaches
$\mathrm{KS}=0.2$. That is the type mixing, not agreement. Restricted to the
owning type, \texttt{theta} reaches $0.678$ with $+1.86$ standard deviations of
displacement, and \texttt{p} widens by a factor of $2.54$. The four type
indicators differ only slightly, which says the two grids have a similar mix of
busbars, assets, and lines per local graph rather than saying anything about
their physics.
\\
\\
The extremes make the same point concretely. \texttt{p} spans $[0, 97]$~MW on
\texttt{bus14} and $[-281, 399]$~MW on WCCI, where $7.8\,\%$ of asset rows are
negative and \texttt{bus14} has none. \texttt{theta} spans $[-11.6, 1.5]$
degrees against $[-7.1, 16.8]$: not a wider version of the same distribution but
a displaced one.

\section{Two failure modes}
\label{sec:wcci-interpretation}

The left panel of Figure~\ref{fig:wcci-offset-scale} separates the channels
along the two axes; the right panel repeats the diagnostic after the corrections
and is read in Section~\ref{sec:wcci-corrected}. The distinction is not
cosmetic, because the architecture responds to the two axes differently.

{\color{clarif}
\paragraph{Reading the axes.} Each point is one channel, placed by the two
statistics of Equation~\eqref{eq:offset-scale} over all sampled entries:
horizontally the offset magnitude $|\mu_B-\mu_A|/\sigma_p$, vertically the scale
ratio $\sigma_B/\sigma_A$ on a log axis so that halving and doubling sit
equidistant from $1$. A channel needing no correction sits at the bottom left,
so distance from that corner is distance from comparability and its direction
names which of the two failures is at work.}
\\
\\

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/wcci_feature_offset_scale.png}
  \caption{Absolute offset against scale ratio, over all sampled entries. The
  shaded band and the dashed line mark a scale ratio within a third of unity;
  the dotted vertical line marks an offset of $0.3$ pooled standard deviations.
  \emph{Left:} as measured. Channels outside those bounds fail in one of two
  distinct ways --- the power channels keep their centre and widen, the angles
  keep their width and move. \emph{Right:} the same diagnostic after the two
  corrections of Section~\ref{sec:wcci-correction}, discussed in
  Section~\ref{sec:wcci-corrected}. Points are placed on the owning node type.
  Every channel now sits inside the scale band and the worst displacement falls
  from $1.86$ to $0.81$, but \texttt{p} acquires an offset it did not have.}
  \label{fig:wcci-offset-scale}
\end{figure}

\noindent The actor's node pre-encoder is a linear map followed by ReLU and LayerNorm. LayerNorm effectively absorbs a uniform change in \emph{scale}, as multiplying inputs by a constant leaves the normalized activations largely unchanged. However, it cannot absorb an \emph{offset}: displacing a channel moves the pre-activations across the ReLU threshold, fundamentally altering which units fire. Post-hoc normalization cannot undo this destroyed activation pattern.
\\
\\
This makes the angle, not the power, the critical failure point. The power channel \texttt{p} differs in width by a factor of $2.54$, which LayerNorm partially self-corrects (though WCCI's deep negative region remains unseen territory). Conversely, \texttt{theta} is displaced by $1.86$ standard deviations, an offset the architecture cannot correct.
\\
\\
\texttt{rho}, meanwhile, needs no correction at all: scale ratio $1.050$, offset
$-0.26$ --- the same values the busbar schema gives, since it is the same set of
lines carrying the same loadings. The single most decision-relevant channel is
already comparable across grids because it was defined in per-unit terms, an
argument for expressing features in grid-relative units wherever the physics
permits it.


\section{Correcting the inputs}
\label{sec:wcci-correction}

Two preprocessing mechanisms exist in the codebase. Deterministic physical
scaling divides each channel by a per-environment physical constant. Running
standardization maintains per-environment, per-node-type statistics over
\texttt{p} and \texttt{theta}, excluding \texttt{rho}, which the measurements
above confirm needs no help. On the diagnosis alone the second looks like the
match, being the only one that can move a displaced channel. It is not.

\subsection{Running standardization makes the mismatch worse}
\label{sec:wcci-running-norm}

Standardizing each environment with its own statistics aligns the aggregate
moments exactly: every channel comes out at offset $0.00$ and scale ratio
$1.00$. It also moves both channels it touches an order of magnitude further
apart (Table~\ref{tab:wcci-running-norm}).

\noindent The cause is the shared-column structure of
Section~\ref{sec:wcci-features}. Subtracting a per-grid mean moves each channel's
point mass at zero to a grid-dependent location, and since three quarters of all
rows are zeros for \texttt{p} and \texttt{theta}, the encoder's single most
frequent input stops meaning the same thing on the two grids --- a larger
mismatch than the displacement being corrected. On \texttt{theta} a row that does
not own the channel reads $+0.481$ on \texttt{bus14} and $-0.143$ on WCCI, so the
same absence lands on opposite sides of zero.
\\
\\
Two further properties rule the mechanism out independently of these numbers.
Standardization is scale-invariant, so it erases any scaling applied before it
--- dividing by a constant and then standardizing reproduces standardizing alone
to four decimals --- meaning the two mechanisms cannot be combined. And running
statistics begin empty and converge during training, so a transferred encoder
would spend early target training reading a distribution that is itself still
moving, where a deterministic scaling is fixed at construction and identical on
both grids from the first step.

\subsection{Physical scaling for the power channels}
\label{sec:wcci-physical-scaling}

Physical scaling, defined in Section~\ref{subsec:graph-feature-normalization},
divides the power channels by each environment's own maximum generator capacity.
Two properties answer the failure diagnosed above: being multiplicative it
leaves zeros at zero, so the point mass stays aligned on both grids, and being
fixed at construction it does not drift the way estimated moments do. The
divisor is a single generator's nameplate rating, a crude proxy for grid scale,
so it is worth checking against the alternatives
(Table~\ref{tab:wcci-normalizer}); it is the closest of the four to the divisor
that would equalize the two spreads exactly.

\subsection{The angle displacement is a gauge artifact}
\label{sec:wcci-angle-representation}

Scaling cannot repair the angle displacement. Absolute voltage angles are referenced to the slack bus, and because the two grids place their slack buses differently, the displacement observed in Figure~\ref{fig:wcci-distributions} is a gauge artifact rather than a physical difference. No rescaling or recentering can fix a reference-dependent quantity; it must be replaced entirely.
\\
\\
The solution is to use the angle drop along each line (Section~\ref{sec:line-node-angle}). As a difference, it is gauge-invariant. On the disaggregated graph, this feature lives on the transmission-line node (the most populous type), gracefully trading a feature owned by a quarter of the rows for one owned by a third. We therefore replace absolute angles with line-angle differences in both environments.

\subsection{The corrected distribution}
\label{sec:wcci-corrected}

Table~\ref{tab:wcci-corrected} measures both changes together, under the same

Figure~\ref{fig:wcci-offset-scale} places the result back on the offset--scale
diagnostic. The angle channel lands in the band occupied by \texttt{rho}, and
the power spread comes within $11\,\%$ of parity.

\noindent The angle correction is unambiguous: replacing a reference-dependent quantity
by a gauge-invariant one takes the worst channel in the chapter from $0.678$ to
$0.230$, and its scale ratio stays near one throughout.
\\
\\
The power correction is a genuine exchange rather than a repair. Dividing by
each grid's largest generator rating brings the spread from $2.54$ to $0.89$,
but the raw means, nearly equal at $-0.158$, are not preserved:
\texttt{bus14} runs its generators at $39\,\%$ of its fleet maximum against
WCCI's $20\,\%$, so a divisor taken from the largest unit lands the two grids at
different normalized levels and the displacement grows to $-0.808$. KS rises
with it, from $0.201$ to $0.511$. Section~\ref{sec:wcci-interpretation} argued
that offsets, not scale errors, are what the architecture cannot absorb, so this
moves \texttt{p} onto the axis that binds.
\\
\\
The exchange is still favourable: the worst displacement across all channels
falls from $1.864$ to $0.808$, every scale ratio ends inside the band, and no
channel remains outside both bounds at once. But it is an exchange, not an
elimination --- a divisor reflecting typical rather than peak generation would
plausibly do better on both axes, and selecting one against the offset as well as
the scale ratio is left open. \latestrevision{The two transfer branches test
whether the residual displacement matters in practice. Chapter~\ref{ch:encoder-transfer-bridge}
compares frozen source encoders trained with the two input conditions, while
Section~\ref{sec:wcci-design} repeats the comparison for the complete
candidate-action policy.}

\begin{tcolorbox}[
  colframe=red!75!black,
  colback=red!5!white,
  title=Chapter 7 Summary: What the Shared Encoder Reads]
\textbf{Purpose:} To measure what the shared encoder actually reads on both
grids, so that a deficit under transfer can be told apart from a representation
reading miscalibrated inputs.
\\
\\
\textbf{Content:} Do-nothing rollouts on both grids ($426{,}000$ and
$1{,}236{,}000$ node observations) compared channel by channel on offset, scale
ratio and KS. The pre-encoder's LayerNorm absorbs a change in
\emph{scale} but not an \emph{offset}, which moves pre-activations across the
ReLU threshold --- so the two axes are not equally serious.
\\
\\
\textbf{Findings:}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \texttt{rho} needs no correction (ratio $1.05$, offset $-0.26$): it is
  already in per-unit terms.
  \item \texttt{theta} is displaced by $1.864\sigma$, a gauge artifact of each
  grid's slack bus. The gauge-invariant angle drop takes it from
  $\mathrm{KS}=0.678$ to $0.230$.
  \item Physical scaling of \texttt{p} is an exchange, not a repair: spread
  $2.54\to0.89$, but offset $-0.158\to-0.808$. The worst displacement overall
  still falls from $1.864$ to $0.808$.
  \item Running standardization is rejected: it aligns the moments exactly yet
  triples KS on \texttt{p} ($0.042\to0.773$), because a per-grid mean displaces
  the point mass at zero that three quarters of all rows carry.
  \item The action descriptor was normalized per agent, so the same action read
  $0.60$ for one agent and $0.50$ for another --- which explains why the
  descriptor-free \texttt{f0} cells were robust in \latestrevision{Screen~F}.
\end{itemize}
\textbf{Takeaway:} Deterministic physical scaling and the edge-difference angle
are adopted, running standardization is not. \latestrevision{Since the power
correction trades a scale error for a displacement, both input conditions are
carried into the frozen-encoder experiment of
Chapter~\ref{ch:encoder-transfer-bridge} and the complete-policy experiment of
Chapter~\ref{ch:screen-f-transfer}.}
\end{tcolorbox}
